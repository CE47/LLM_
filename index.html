How a Chatbot Learns
Step 1 · Getting the data

First, gather a mountain of text

Books, websites, articles, code, subtitles. Nobody labels it. It is just plain text — trillions of words of it.

0 tokens of text read by the model
Real number: Meta trained its Llama 3 model on about 15 trillion tokens of text. A token is a small chunk of text — more on that in the next step.
Step 2 · Tokenizing

Chop the text into tokens

Computers do not read letters or words. Text gets cut into pieces called tokens — and every token is stored as a number.

text of the token  ·  the number the computer stores
Rule of thumb: one token is about 3 of 4 of a word in English. Real models keep a fixed list of roughly 100,000 possible tokens. (This demo's chopper is simplified — real ones are learned from data.)
Step 3 · Pre-training

Learn by guessing the next token

Hide the next token. Guess it. Check the real answer. Nudge the model a little. Repeat trillions of times.

Why this works: the text is its own answer key, so it can practise on trillions of examples with no human labelling anything. To guess well, it ends up learning grammar, facts and patterns. This stage is called pre-training — it takes months and thousands of specialized chips.
Step 4 · The base model

Now it knows language — but it is not helpful yet

The model got extremely good at finishing text. It has no idea it is supposed to answer you.

What is the capital of France?
It did not answer. It just continued the pattern.
A model at this point is called a base model. It is a text-continuation machine, not an assistant. So how do we teach it to help?
Step 5 · Fine-tuning

Show it thousands of good answers

Humans write example conversations: here is a question, here is a great answer. The model copies the shape of being helpful.

This stage is called fine-tuning (or instruction tuning). It uses thousands of examples, not trillions — a tiny fraction of the work of pre-training.
Step 6 · Learning from feedback

Let people pick the better answer

Two answers. One person. They click the one they prefer. Do that again and again, and the model learns what people actually want.

Leans toward answers people prefer 0%
This stage is usually called RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback. People do not rewrite the model; they only say "this one was better". Those choices steer it toward being helpful, honest and safe.
Step 7 · Testing and safety

Test it hard, then lock it

Thousands of test questions. Trick questions designed to break it. Safety checks. Fix what fails, then freeze the model.

    Locked

    The numbers inside the model are now frozen. It is copied onto servers and switched on.

    Real fact: testing never really ends. Even after a model ships, teams keep hunting for new ways to break it.

    The big picture

    Four things worth remembering

    You just walked through the whole pipeline: pre-training → fine-tuning → human feedback → testing → inference.

    1

    Trained once, used forever

    Training sets the numbers inside the model. After that they are frozen. Every time you chat with it, you are using the same frozen model.

    2

    It predicts, it does not look up

    It always guesses the most likely next token. Sounding likely is not the same as being true — that is why it can be confidently wrong.

    3

    Randomness is a dial

    Low temperature: it almost always picks the safest token. High temperature: it takes risks. At 0 it always picks the single most likely token.

    4

    It forgets you

    It only sees the text inside the current conversation, up to a size limit called the context window. Nothing you say retrains it.

    One more honest thing: the little model in Step 8 has hand-made numbers so you could watch the guessing. Real models learn their numbers from data — but the guessing step works exactly the same way.
